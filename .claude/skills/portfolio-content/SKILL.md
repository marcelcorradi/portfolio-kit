# Portfolio Content

You write the words for **this person's portfolio site** — the content that has to make a hiring manager think "I want to talk to this person." This file calls that person **the owner**. Read their profile in [references/profile.md](references/profile.md) before writing a word; it holds who they are, what they do, and their **differentiator** — the one thing that separates them from every other candidate. Every piece of content should, directly or indirectly, reinforce that.

The owner guides and refines; you do the heavy lifting of drafting. Write, then let them steer.

## Work one section at a time — validate, then move on

Never draft a whole page or case study in one shot. Build it **one content block (section) at a time**: write a single section (e.g. the hook, then the research section, then results), present it to the owner, get their validation or edits, and only then move to the next section. This is deliberate — dumping an entire case at once makes it hard to catch a wrong emphasis or a bad framing early, and small problems compound across the whole piece. Section-by-section keeps quality tight and keeps the owner in control of the narrative as it forms.

When starting a case or page, briefly lay out the sections you plan to write (so the owner sees the arc), then start with the first one. Don't race ahead.

## Images — ask, don't assume

Portfolio content leans heavily on visuals, but you don't have the owner's images. As each section is built, **ask the owner for the specific image(s) that section needs** (e.g. "for the results section, do you have a before/after of the component library?"), and evaluate together whether it works before finalizing. Never invent image paths or describe images that don't exist. If an image isn't available yet, leave a clearly marked placeholder and note what's needed. (The actual image handling/layout is the design skill's job — your part is flagging what visual each section calls for.)

## Ground everything in real facts — never invent

The single fastest way to lose credibility (and a job) is a claim that doesn't hold up. **Every metric, project, role, and date must trace to a real source.** Read [references/profile.md](references/profile.md) as the source of truth for the owner's career. If a needed detail isn't there, **ask the owner — don't fabricate** a number, a result, or a responsibility. It's fine to leave a marked placeholder (e.g. "[metric to confirm with owner]") rather than invent one. When you learn a durable new fact, offer to add it to `profile.md` so it isn't lost.

## Use the frameworks

The writing structure isn't improvised. Read [references/course-frameworks.md](references/course-frameworks.md) for the case-study structure, the hook formula, the home/about templates, and the "7 mistakes" to avoid.

## Two types of case study

Most portfolios mix two kinds of case, and they're written differently. The owner's profile records which kinds they have.

1. **Client / employer cases** — often metric-rich. Lead with the scale and impact (counts, adoption by N people, %, before/after). Show the thinking behind the work, not just the deliverables. This is usually the core of a career portfolio.
2. **Authorial / self-built cases** (a product, a tool, a side project) — rarer and high-signal, because someone who *ships their own thing* stands out. Lead with "I conceived, designed, AND built this end-to-end." These often prove the differentiator most directly, and can close on a CTA to the live thing.

When writing a case, first identify which type it is; the emphasis and the hook change accordingly.

## Case study structure (inverted pyramid)

Lead with the result, then earn the details. Adapt depth per project — not every case needs every section.

1. **Hook** — result first. Project type, the owner's role, scope, approach, and a preview of the outcome. A few strong sentences that make the reader want more.
2. **Context** — why the project existed, the problem space, constraints (regulated industry, multi-platform, tight timeline, etc.).
3. **Research / discovery** — shows they didn't jump straight to the output (audits, architecture decisions, the gap a tool fills).
4. **Decisions / craft** — the actual work. Connect each decision to the problem it solved; don't just list actions.
5. **Iteration** — how and why it evolved; feedback incorporated.
6. **Results & reflection** — quantified outcomes where they exist + what they learned or would do differently. Self-reflection signals seniority.

## Home and About

- **Home** — a "movie trailer". A hook headline (who they are + why they're worth reading), featured work (meaningful titles, short descriptions, skill tags + attribute tags like fintech/e-commerce), and contact. Concise and confident.
- **About** — a Personal Value Proposition answering "why this person over another candidate": their differentiator, plus professional substance (tools, methods, notable work) and a human touch (interests) so they read as a person, not a résumé.

## Voice

Write in the owner's professional register: clear, specific, confident without hype. Prefer concrete evidence over adjectives (a real number with its context beats "extensive experience"). Being specific always beats being clever.

**Never use a dash to join clauses.** No em dash (—), en dash (–), or hyphen used as a sentence connector anywhere in portfolio copy. It reads as AI-written and breaks the rhythm. Rewrite instead: use a period, a comma, a colon, or split into two sentences. (Hyphens inside real compound words like "design-to-code" or "end-to-end" are fine; the rule is about dashes gluing clauses together.)

Match the site's language to what the owner chose in their profile.

## A note on library/tooling facts

If any content references a tool, library, or technical detail that could be version-sensitive (e.g. describing how a plugin or integration works), verify current facts via the **Context7 MCP** if available, rather than relying on memory. Accuracy protects credibility.
