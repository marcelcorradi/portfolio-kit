# Course Frameworks — Recruiter-Optimized Portfolio Writing

Distilled from established portfolio-writing guidance for landing design and product roles. Use these as the writing structure so content is proven, not improvised.

## The core principle: inverted pyramid

Lead with the result/outcome, *then* provide the context and process. A hiring manager decides in seconds whether to keep reading — give them the payoff first. This applies to case intros, the home, and each case section.

## Case study structure (narrative, not a task list)

Adapt depth per project — not every case needs every section. Connect each decision to the problem it solved; never just "I did X, then Y".

1. **Hook** — result first. Project type, the owner's role, scope, approach/methodology, and a preview of the outcome. A few strong sentences.
2. **Research / discovery** — shows they understood the users/problem before producing anything (audits, architecture, the gap a tool fills). Signals they didn't jump straight to the output.
3. **Sketches / wireframes / decisions** — information hierarchy, how research became structure, the actual craft.
4. **Prototyping / iteration** — skill on display, how and why it evolved, feedback incorporated.
5. **Results & lessons** — quantified outcome where it exists + self-reflection (what worked, what they'd do differently). Self-reflection signals seniority.

### Hook formula (case intro)

Make the reader want more. Include: **project type** (client/product/authorial), **scope** (full process or a slice), **role** (solo/team, how organized), **approach/methodology** (and why it fit), and a **preview of results** (a few key screens, a metric, a demo). Example shape: *"How [X action] led to [Y outcome]? Here's how I [did the thing]."* Keep it concise and concrete.

## Home page ("movie trailer")

Make the visitor sure they've found the right portfolio and shouldn't click away.

- **Nav** — name clearly, simple structure.
- **Hook** — concise, impactful headline; quick who-they-are; optional professional photo.
- **Featured work** — thumbnails, meaningful titles (not "My first project"), a line or two of description, **skill tags** (e.g. design tokens, user research) + **attribute tags** (fintech, e-commerce, B2B). Whole card clickable, hover feedback.
- **Contact** — email and key links reachable.
- Advanced (as it grows): testimonials, expertise areas (no progress bars — they mean nothing objective), awards, community involvement.

## About Me

Balance professionalism and personality; it's like a friend introducing the owner to an employer.

- **Personal Value Proposition** — "why this person over another candidate": their differentiator, the problems they solve, what's unique about them.
- **Professional** — role/specialization, relevant experience (highlights, not everything), tools/methods, community involvement.
- **Personal** — hobbies, interests, causes — enough to humanize, chosen for relevance.

## The 7 mistakes to avoid (also the orchestrator's quality bar)

1. **Poor time management** — don't polish non-essentials (a personal logo); invest in strong case studies.
2. **Quantity over quality** — show only the best; one weak case drags the perceived level of all.
3. **Lack of curation** — align projects to the target role; cut off-target work.
4. **Carelessness** — no typos, grammar errors, or misalignment; the portfolio is itself a work sample.
5. **Lack of personalization** — don't use a popular template unmodified; the "wow" dies when everyone uses it.
6. **No self-reflection** — include learnings and critical thinking, not just tools/methods used.
7. **Not following tool standards** — for web: responsive + accessible.

## Show, don't tell (visual case studies)

Treat each case study *as a design project*: show work in context, clear scope up front (type, role, timeframe, tools), cohesive visual identity, before/after comparisons ("from ambiguous to obvious"), consistent UI/typography/spacing. A polished case *itself* demonstrates design skill.

## How many cases

Beginners ≥3 strong projects; intermediate 5–6; even one great case can land interviews. Quality over quantity, always. A senior portfolio should lead with its strongest work and use authorial/self-built projects to prove the differentiator.
